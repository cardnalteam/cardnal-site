The mark is a stylised wing built from two red planes. It is the only piece of Cardnal that can never be redrawn, re-angled, or re-coloured. Everything else in this system is derived from it.

Clear space

Reserve clear space equal to the height of the mark's lower wing — roughly 40% of the mark's total height — on all four sides. Nothing enters it: no text, no rules, no image edges, no other logos. It scales with the mark, so it never needs recalculating.

Below the screen minimum the notch between the two wings closes up and the mark reads as a smudge. When you are below it, the answer is the icon at a larger size — never a shrunken lockup.

The 26.7° rule

The top edge of the mark sits at exactly 26.7°. That angle is the system's structural constant — it sets the rake of light across the recommendation card, the direction content enters on a swap, and the diagonal in marketing artwork. When a design needs an angle, it uses this one.

The red-on-red problem, and how we solve it

Our brand colour is red. The universal colour for errors is also red. Left alone, users cannot tell a primary action from a failure. Our rule: vivid brand red (--red-500) is reserved for brand marks and primary actions only. Errors use Alert (--alert, #A61B2B) — noticeably darker and cooler — and always carry an icon and a text label, never colour alone. A destructive button is Alert, never brand red.

Contrast, verified

Every pairing below was measured, not eyeballed. WCAG AA requires 4.5:1 for body text and 3:1 for large text and meaningful graphics.

The 60/30/10 discipline

On any Cardnal screen: roughly 60% white or near-white surface, 30% ink for type and structure, and no more than 10% red. If a screen has two red elements competing, one of them is not the primary action and should be secondary or ghost.

Typography

Three faces, three jobs, no exceptions. Archivo carries voice, Instrument Sans carries information, JetBrains Mono carries data and system labels.

Archivo · Display · 500 600 700 800

Instrument Sans · Body · 400 500 600

JetBrains Mono · Data · 400 500 700

Why these three

Archivo is a grotesque with a slightly narrow, high-pressure feel — it holds tight negative letter-spacing at display sizes without falling apart, which is what makes the headline feel engineered rather than generic. Instrument Sans is quieter and more open, so long explanations stay comfortable. JetBrains Mono has tabular figures, so a column of reward rates aligns on the decimal without extra work.

Why not Inter

Inter is the default for every fintech and dev-tool site built in the last five years. It is an excellent typeface and it would make Cardnal look like everyone else. Instrument Sans gets the same neutrality with slightly more character in the a, g, and t.

Rules

  • Tracking tightens as size grows. Display is −4.5%, body is 0. Never track out headings.
  • Sentence case everywhere except Overline, which is uppercase mono. No Title Case Headlines.
  • One display face per screen. If two things are both H1, neither is.
  • Numbers that represent money or rewards use JetBrains Mono, so they align in columns and read as data.
  • Line length caps at 70 characters for body, 62 for lead paragraphs.
  • Never use faux bold or faux italic. Load the real weight or pick another.

Rhythm rules

  • Never use an arbitrary value. If 8 is too tight and 16 too loose, the problem is the layout, not the scale.

Prefer a border to a shadow

On a white product surface, a 1px --line border separates things more cleanly than a shadow and costs nothing in render time. Reach for elevation only when something genuinely floats above the page: a dropdown, a modal, a toast, or the extension panel sitting over a merchant's checkout.

Rules

The 26.7° swap

The one piece of motion unique to Cardnal. When a recommendation changes — new merchant, new category, new card — content leaves and arrives along the angle of the mark rather than sliding straight across. It is subtle at 9px of travel, and it is the brand moving.

Reduced motion is not optional

Every animation sits behind @media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce). Under that query, transitions drop to 0.01ms and the card swap becomes a straight cross-fade. Nothing loses function.

The Recommendation Card

One component matters more than the rest of the system combined. Everything Cardnal promises is delivered by this card, so it gets its own specification.

Prime VisaVISA
5%back
Highest rewards for Amazon purchases.

Anatomy, in priority order

#ElementRule
1The rateLargest element on the card. Archivo 800, 42px, mono-aligned. This is the answer.
2The card name16px semibold. Must match what is printed on the physical card.
3The reasonOne sentence, under 60 characters, above the fold. Never truncated.
4The networkMono, 10px, low contrast. Confirmation, not information.
5Light rakeA single highlight at 26.7° across the surface. The brand's fingerprint.

Non-negotiables

  • A recommendation never appears without its reason. If we cannot explain it in one sentence, we do not show it.
  • The rate is green, not red. Red is the brand and the action; earnings are gains.
  • Ties are shown as ties. Two cards within 0.5% both appear, with the tiebreaker named.
  • No merchant logos on the card. Trademark risk, and it makes us look like an affiliate.
  • The card never covers the checkout button in the extension. It anchors above or beside.

12

Illustration

The answer is minimal geometric — flat, two-colour, built from the mark's own angles. Not 3D. Not glassmorphism.

The decision, and why

StyleVerdictReasoning
Minimal geometricChosenDerives from the logo, renders at any size, costs nothing to produce, and stays out of the way of information. Scales as we add surfaces.
Flat character illustrationNoReads friendly-startup rather than premium, and it dates fast. Every fintech had it in 2019.
3D renderNoExpensive, slow to iterate, and impossible to keep consistent across a small team. Also the current default for crypto.
GlassmorphismNeverBlur and transparency reduce contrast, which fails the accessibility floor we set in section 02. It is also a trend with a visible expiry date.

Construction rules

  • Built from rectangles, circles, and lines at 26.7° or 90°. No other angles.
  • Two colours maximum per illustration: one ink value plus brand red, or ink plus white.
  • Stroke weights match the icon system: 1.75px at standard scale.
  • No gradients, no drop shadows, no texture. Flat fills only.
  • Illustrations carry meaning — a diagram of how routing works, not a decorative blob.
Where illustration is allowed

Empty states, onboarding steps, blog headers, and explainer diagrams. Not in the recommendation flow. When someone is about to pay, the interface shows data and nothing else.

Voice & Tone

Cardnal speaks like a knowledgeable friend who respects your time: short sentences, plain verbs, no hedging, no hype. We state the answer, then the reason.

The four rules

  1. Lead with the answer. The recommendation comes first, the explanation second.
  2. Always give the reason. A recommendation without a "because" is a guess as far as the user knows.
  3. Never oversell. We say 5% back. We do not say unlock incredible rewards.
  4. Own failures plainly. Say what happened and what to do. Do not apologise twice.

Brand Personality

Cardnal in five words: Decisive. Clear. Precise. Discreet. Useful.

01Decisive

We give one answer. Design consequence: one primary button per screen, one recommendation per checkout, no dashboards of options.

02Clear

Every claim carries its reason. Design consequence: the reason line is never truncated, never behind a tooltip, never optional.

03Precise

Numbers are exact and sourced. Design consequence: mono figures, consistent decimals, issuer terms as the citation.

04Discreet

We appear at the moment of payment and then leave. Design consequence: no badges begging for attention, no daily engagement notifications.

05Useful

Utility over delight. Design consequence: we cut an animation before we cut a millisecond of speed.

How the traits arbitrate

When two good options conflict, the earlier trait wins. A screen that is beautiful but offers three equal choices loses to a plainer screen that gives one answer — Decisive outranks everything. When a designer asks "should we show more here?", the honest answer is almost always no.

15

Brand Story

Our story

Most people carry three to five credit cards. Each one is best at something — this one at groceries, that one at restaurants, another at travel — and the differences are worth real money over a year. Almost nobody keeps track. At the counter, in the two seconds before paying, people reach for whichever card is in front.

Cardnal started from a plain observation: the information needed to make that decision already exists, it is just never present at the moment it matters. The rewards are published. The categories are known. The card is in the wallet. The only missing piece is someone telling you, right then, which one to use.

So we built that. Not a budgeting app, not a bank, not another card to apply for — a layer of intelligence sitting between a person and the payment they are already about to make.

Our mission

Help people get more from the cards they already own.

Note what the mission does not say. It does not say help people get more cards. Every product decision is measured against whether it makes an existing wallet work harder.

Our vision

To become the intelligence layer between every purchase and every payment method. Google Maps tells you the best route. Cardnal tells you the best way to pay.

The extension is the first surface, not the destination. The path runs through mobile, wallet integrations, spending insights, and eventually smart payment routing — one card that decides, at the moment of tap, which of your cards actually pays.

Why Cardnal exists

  • Because the rewards are already yours. This is not a new financial product. It is unclaimed value sitting in wallets people already carry.
  • Because the decision is genuinely hard. Rotating categories, caps, multipliers, and transfer partners change quarterly. No reasonable person should be expected to track this.
  • Because the moment is specific. Advice a week before a purchase is trivia. Advice two seconds before is worth money.
  • Because nobody has made it simple. The tools that exist are spreadsheets, forums, and blogs written for hobbyists. This should work for someone who has never heard the phrase points optimisation.

Brand Pillars

Five pillars. Every product, design, and marketing decision should visibly support at least one, and never contradict any.

Pillar 01Answer, don't advise

We give a decision, not a comparison table. If the interface leaves the user still choosing, it has failed at its only job.

Pillar 02Show the reason

Every recommendation carries a plain-language because. A black box that is right is worth less than a glass box that is right.

Pillar 03Trust over rewards

Where maximising rewards conflicts with the user's interest — pushing a card with a fee they will not recoup — we lose the rewards.

Pillar 04Arrive at the moment

Value is delivered at checkout, not in a weekly digest. We measure success by decisions improved, not sessions opened.

Pillar 05Earn the wallet

We touch the most sensitive thing a person owns. Security, data minimisation, and clarity about what we store come before any growth tactic.

Using the pillars in review

In design and product review, name the pillar. "This supports Pillar 01 because it removes the comparison step." A feature that supports none of the five is a feature for a different company.

Product Principles

The pillars say what we stand for. These principles say what to do on Tuesday when two reasonable engineers disagree.

1. Simple before powerful

Ship the version a first-time user understands without explanation, then add depth behind progressive disclosure. Power that costs comprehension is not power, it is debt.

In practice: The MVP shows one card, one rate, one reason. Alternatives sit one tap away, not on the surface.

2. Trust before growth

We will decline growth tactics that borrow against trust: dark patterns, pre-checked consent, cards recommended because they pay us more, or notifications engineered for habit rather than utility.

In practice: If affiliate revenue and the best recommendation ever diverge, the recommendation wins and the divergence gets disclosed.

3. Explain every recommendation

The reason is a product requirement, not a nicety. If the system cannot articulate why in one sentence, the recommendation is not ready to ship.

In practice: The reason string is a required field in the recommendation schema. No default, no null.

4. Never overwhelm

Every additional element on the payment screen costs attention at the exact moment attention is scarcest. Additions must earn their place by removing something else.

In practice: A hard cap of one recommendation plus three alternatives. Beyond that we are a comparison site.

5. Speed matters

A recommendation that arrives after the user has already paid is worth nothing. Latency is a correctness property here, not a performance nice-to-have.

In practice: Budget of 200ms from merchant detection to rendered recommendation. Card rules cached client-side; the network is an enhancement, not a dependency.

6. The wallet is the user's, not ours

We store the minimum that makes recommendations work. We do not need card numbers to know that a Prime Visa earns 5% at Amazon — so we do not ask for them.

In practice: Card identity, not card credentials. Say so on the marketing site, in the onboarding, and in the privacy policy, in the same words.

7. Be right, then be fast, then be pretty

The order is not negotiable. A beautiful wrong recommendation damages the brand more than a plain right one ever could.

Applying these to engineering decisions

These principles have architectural consequences. Principle 5 argues for a client-side rules engine with a cached ruleset. Principle 6 argues against storing PANs, which keeps us out of PCI scope entirely at this stage. Principle 3 argues for a rules-based recommendation core — a model that cannot explain itself cannot satisfy the requirement.

Americans leave billions in credit card rewards unclaimed every year, not because the rewards are hidden but because the decision happens at the worst possible moment — standing at a counter, or one click from checkout, with no way to remember that this card does 5% here and that one does 1%.

Waitlist page

  • Email field and button on one row at ≥980px, stacked below.

The dark-mode red trap

--red-500 on --ink-950 measures 4.1:1 — it fails AA for body text. In dark mode, red text must use --red-400 (6.0:1). The button fill stays --red-500 because white on it still passes. Elevation in dark mode is expressed by surface lightness, not shadow.
