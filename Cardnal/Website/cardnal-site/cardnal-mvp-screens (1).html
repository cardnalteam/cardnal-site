MVP Product Screens

Ten screens. One promise.

Tell Cardnal what's in your wallet and what you value. Cardnal figures out what to use. Everything here serves that sentence. Anything that doesn't, isn't in the MVP.

Rules

  • Google first. It's one tap and it removes password creation from the funnel entirely. Email second, for people who won't use social login.
  • No "Sign in" / "Sign up" split. Both buttons do the right thing depending on whether the account exists. A user shouldn't have to remember which they are.
  • The privacy line is on this screen, not buried in Terms. "We never ask for card numbers" is the objection everyone has, answered before they can raise it.
  • No password field, no confirm-password, no email verification gate before value. Verify later, in the background.

Why this design

  • "Most common" list before search. A blank search box asks the user to do work. A list of the twenty most-held cards means many people never type at all — they just tap four rows.
  • The button counts. "Add 4 cards" confirms what's about to happen and updates live. At zero it reads "Add your cards" and is disabled.
  • Annual fee is visible at selection. It's part of how we'll rank later, so showing it now makes the later maths feel consistent rather than surprising.
  • One card is enough to continue. Don't gate on three. A user with one card still gets a working product, and they'll add more once they trust it.
The hardest problem on this screen isn't visual

It's card identification. "Chase Sapphire" could be Preferred or Reserve, and they earn differently. Search must handle nicknames, partial names, and misspellings, and where two products are close, the row should show the distinguishing detail — annual fee is usually the fastest tell. Budget real engineering time here; a wrong card silently produces wrong recommendations forever.

Behaviour

AspectSpec
SelectionSingle-select. Radio semantics — tapping a new row deselects the old one. Rows are role="radio" inside role="radiogroup".
Pre-selectionNone. A pre-checked default would be us choosing for them, and the whole point is that they chose.
SkippableNo, but only because it costs one tap. If skipped by any future path, defaultCardId is null and all dollar deltas are suppressed product-wide.
One card in walletScreen is skipped entirely. That card becomes the default silently — there is nothing to choose.
Changing laterSettings → My Cards → row action. Changing it does not recompute history; it only affects future recommendations.
Why not just infer the default from usage?

Because we can't see usage — we have no transaction data and deliberately aren't asking for any. Inferring a default from "most-used card" would require exactly the bank connection this MVP refuses to build. Asking is one tap and it's honest about what we know.

What the default is not

It is not "the card Cardnal recommends by default", and it must never be described that way in copy. It is the user's own habit, captured so we can measure against it. If the default is also the best card for a purchase, we say so plainly rather than manufacturing a gain.

Interaction

  • Ranked, not exclusive. Drag to order up to three. Position sets weight — 1st counts most, 3rd counts least.
  • "Best overall value" is exclusive. Selecting it clears the others; it is the "just work it out" option, so ranking it alongside a preference contradicts itself.
  • Ranking "Welcome bonuses" opens the progress sheet immediately, because the goal does nothing without it. It's skippable — the goal then simply has nothing to act on.
  • Default for a skipped screen is a single goal: best overall value, with cross-currency comparison off. Cash and points then rank separately, exactly as in v1.1.
  • No goal is irreversible. Settings → Goals, same screen, same interaction.
Preferences bend the ranking — they don't break it

Someone who prefers points still wants to know when a cashback card earns dramatically more. If the objectively best card beats the preference-weighted winner by 2× (same currency) or 3× (across currencies), Cardnal recommends the objective winner anyway and says so: "Normally we'd suggest your Amex, but this earns 3× more here." That published threshold is what "don't recommend my Amex unless it's substantially better" actually means in code.

Two goals need a decision before this screen is final

Card offers are entirely user-declared, change weekly, and have no structured source. A goal the product can barely honour is worse than one fewer goal — consider cutting it from the launch set. Best overall value requires converting points to dollars, which the engine otherwise refuses to do. It can ship, but only as an opt-in with a published, user-adjustable valuation shown wherever a converted figure appears. See Recommendation Engine §09 and §10.

How the same purchase gets four different answers

$200 at a restaurant. Wallet: Amex Gold (4x points), Card B (5% cash), Card C (2% cash, $300 left on an active bonus).

Never guess an order total

The total is read from the rendered page on merchants where the selector is mapped and verified. It is never inferred from cart contents, never estimated, and never carried over from a previous page. If the value fails validation — not a currency, implausible magnitude, changed between two reads 300ms apart — treat it as unknown and fall back to variant B. A wrong dollar figure at checkout costs more trust than a missing one.

Copy rules

  • Imperative, naming the card: "Use your Prime Visa." Not "We recommend", not "Consider".
  • Rate and amount always travel together. "5% back on this $284.00 purchase" — a bare percentage is abstract.
  • Round the delta down to the cent. Under-promising is the only safe direction.
  • "about" prefixes any figure derived from an estimated rate: "about $11". Exact rates get exact figures.
  • No exclamation marks. No "Save now", "Don't miss", or any urgency construction.

Why this cardCash and points ranked separately

The rule that changed

We no longer value points at 1¢

The previous draft converted points to dollars at a flat penny each. That is a valuation judgement dressed up as a fact — a Sapphire point transferred to a partner can be worth 2¢, and redeemed for cash it can be worth 1¢ or less. Publishing a single number implies a precision we don't have. Until a documented valuation methodology exists and the user has opted into it, Cardnal ranks cash against cash and points against points, and never crosses the two.

ComparisonBehaviour
Cashback vs. cashbackRank by dollars. Show the delta. This is the only case that produces a dollar figure.
Points vs. points, same currencyRank by multiplier and points earned. "4x vs. 1x — 852 more points."
Points vs. points, different currenciesShow both, rank neither. "Amex Gold earns 4x MR. Sapphire earns 3x UR." Let the user decide.
Cashback vs. pointsNo cross-comparison. Two lists, both shown, winner declared only within each.
Which card gets recommended when both exist?The cashback winner, because its value is certain — with a line noting the points alternative. Certainty over theoretical maximum.

Transparency requirements

  • Every card in the wallet appears, including the ones that lose badly. Hiding losers looks like hiding something.
  • Classification confidence is stated when it isn't high — "Category matched from the merchant domain" vs. "Best guess from the page title".
  • Data age is shown in the footnote. If it exceeds 7 days, it moves out of the footnote into a visible amber notice.
  • Assumptions are named: annual fees excluded, taxes and shipping included as the merchant reported them, promotional rates not modelled.
  • No "apply for a better card" link. Ever, on this screen or any other.

Copy

CaseText
Delta ≥ $1, cashbackYour {card} earns {rate} back here — about ${delta} more than your default.

Rules

  • No urgency language. No "Don't checkout yet", no "Wait", no "Before you pay", no countdown, no exclamation marks. The gain is the argument.
  • Fires at most once per merchant per day, and never more than once per checkout.
  • Only when the delta clears $1.00, or when there's no total but the rate difference is at least 2×.
  • Silent. No sound, no badge count, never on a locked screen.
  • Tapping opens screen 07, not a marketing page. We made a claim; the tap shows the evidence.
  • Two dismissals on one merchant stops it permanently for that merchant, and we tell the user that's what happened.
  • Permission is requested after the first successful in-page recommendation, never at install.
Notifications are the failure path, not the main path

If notifications are firing often, the in-page panel isn't working. Track the ratio: a healthy product shows the panel and stays quiet. Treat a rising notification rate as a bug signal rather than an engagement win.

The cap case needs data we don't have

Knowing that Blue Cash has hit its $6,000 grocery cap requires knowing what the user spent — which means either bank data (out of scope) or the user telling us. Recommendation object — the frontend contract

// everything the UI needs; nothing it has to derive
{
  status: 'ok' | 'no_merchant' | 'no_rule' | 'all_equal' | 'stale' | 'error',
  merchant:   { name, domain, confidence: 'high'|'medium'|'low' },
  category:   { id, label, source: 'domain'|'path'|'title' },
  orderTotal: { cents, currency, source, confident: boolean } | null,
  winner:     { cardId, rate, unit: 'percent'|'multiplier', currency: 'cash'|'UR'|'MR'|…,
                earned: { cents } | { points }, reason: string },
  alternates: [ …same shape ],
  tie:        { isTie: boolean, tiebreaker: string | null },
  vsDefault:  { comparable: boolean, deltaCents: number | null,
                defaultIsWinner: boolean, suppressedReason: string | null },
  goals:      { applied: ['welcome_bonus', 'points'], satisfiedBy: cardId | null,
                overrodePreference: boolean, overrideMultiple: number | null,
                valuationUsed: { perPoint, currency } | null },
  bonus:      { active: boolean, remainingCents, daysLeft, wouldComplete: boolean } | null,
  data:       { rulesetVersion, fetchedAt, ageDays }
}
  • reason is required and non-null on every card. Enforce it in the type, not a lint rule.
  • vsDefault.comparable is false whenever the winner and default use different currencies. The UI reads this flag; it never decides comparability itself.
  • goals.overrodePreference means the objective winner beat the preferred card by more than the override margin. When true, the explanation must acknowledge it — silently contradicting a stated preference is the fastest way to make the goals screen feel fake.
  • goals.valuationUsed is non-null only for best_value, and whenever it is non-null the UI must display the rate used next to any converted figure.
  • suppressedReason carries why a delta is hidden — 'below_threshold', 'no_total', 'cross_currency', 'stale', 'no_default'. Useful in QA, never shown to the user.

Open questions for engineering

  1. Checkout detection heuristic. URL patterns plus DOM signals — needs a defined, testable rule per launch merchant rather than a general guess.
  2. Order-total selector maintenance. Merchants change markup. Who owns the breakage alert, and what's the fallback SLA?
  3. Ruleset distribution. Bundled with the extension, or fetched on install and refreshed daily? Affects the stale-data path.
  4. Card catalog size at launch. The top ~200 consumer cards, or a narrower set with clean data? Narrower is probably right.
  5. Does "Card offers" ship at all? Fully user-declared, weekly-volatile, no structured source. Four goals that work beat five where one is theatre.
  6. Who owns the points valuation table? Choosing 1.0¢ vs 0.6¢ is an editorial call users will argue with. Same problem as reward-rule authorship, same need for an owner.
Approval gate

This document is the specification, not the build. Approve the ten screens, the delta suppression rules, the cash-vs-points separation, and the goal weighting with its override margins before any production code is written — those four decisions shape the data model and are expensive to reverse later. The goals model in particular changes UserState, the recommendation object, and every explanation string.

Flow & Scope

Welcome→ Add cards→ Set default→ Choose goals→ Wallet→ Install extension→ Checkout→ Why?

Three screens carry the product now: Add cards, Choose goals, and Checkout. Add cards tells us what you have, goals tells us what you want, checkout is where both pay off. If checkout is excellent and either input screen is painful, nobody reaches it.

The promise, evolved

v1.1 said: I tell Cardnal which cards I own → I shop → Cardnal tells me which one to use. v1.2 says: Tell Cardnal what's in your wallet and what you value. Cardnal figures out what to use. The second sentence is a harder product to build and a much more defensible one — a rate database can be copied in a weekend; a system that knows you're saving Chase points for travel and chasing an Amex bonus until September cannot.

One screen I'd add to your eight

"Set your default card" — a single question right after Add Cards. It costs one tap and it's what makes the "+$11.36 vs. your default" line possible. Without a deliberately chosen default, that number is comparing against something the user never picked, and the most persuasive element in the product becomes arbitrary.
